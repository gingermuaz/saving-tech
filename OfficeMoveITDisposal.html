Office move IT disposal: what to do with the equipment you're leaving behind

Relocating or closing a site? This guide covers how to audit, decommission and clear redundant IT before the removal vans arrive - with certified data destruction and the documentation to close out the building.

An office move is a data protection event, not just a logistics one

An office move or site closure is one of those events that concentrates years of accumulated IT equipment into a single, time-pressured moment. Devices that have been sitting in comms rooms, storage cupboards, and desk drawers suddenly need to be accounted for, and decisions that should have been made months earlier get made in the final days before the removal vans arrive.

Office move IT disposal is not simply a tidy-up task. It is a data protection and compliance event, and it needs to be planned with the same rigour as the physical relocation itself. Secure data destruction, office move logistics, and certified WEEE compliance all require coordination - not left to chance on removal day. This guide follows the timeline of a move or closure, from planning through to the final documentation that closes out the building. Our article on disposing of old IT assets and avoiding the skip covers the broader compliance picture if you are new to the obligations involved.

Why does IT equipment so often get handled badly during a move or closure?

What gets forgotten in comms rooms, storage cupboards and desk drawers?

The equipment that causes the most problems is rarely the obvious stuff. The laptops on desks and the servers in the main rack tend to get planned for. Sometimes other IT equipment is forgotten: the decommissioned switches and routers sitting in the comms room that never quite made it onto the disposal schedule, the monitors stacked in a storage cupboard from the last refresh cycle, the multifunction printer in the kitchen that has been scanning documents for three years and has a hard drive full of cached data, the desk drawers with USB drives, old phones, and backup tapes in them.

A thorough IT audit before packing begins is the only reliable way to surface this equipment. Our recycling checklist is a useful starting point for taking stock of what you actually have before you start allocating it to move, reuse, or retire.

Why is moving day the worst possible time to be making disposal decisions?

Moving day decisions tend to be made under pressure, by people who are focused on the van, the new office, and the deadline. Equipment that does not have a clear destination gets left behind, given to the removal company to deal with, or put in a skip. All three of these outcomes create risk. Left-behind equipment may end up with the landlord or a general clearance firm that is not authorised to handle data-bearing devices. Equipment given to removal companies may be mishandled or disposed of through channels that generate no documentation. Equipment in a skip is a WEEE office clearance failure, a potential data breach, and leaves your business with nothing to show an auditor.

Forward planning is needed. The time to decide what is being disposed of, and how, is six to eight weeks before the move.

What are the risks of leaving equipment for the landlord or a general clearance firm?

General clearance firms are not authorised WEEE processors, and they are not equipped to carry out secure data destruction. If data-bearing equipment passes through their hands, your organisation's GDPR obligation does not pass with it. The responsibility for the data on that equipment remains yours until you can evidence secure destruction. A general clearance firm cannot give you a Certificate of Data Destruction, and without one you have no evidence that your obligation was met.

How does redundant IT feed into dilapidations and lease exit costs?

IT equipment left in a building at lease exit can trigger dilapidations claims. Landlords are entitled to recover the cost of removing items that should have been cleared by the outgoing tenant, and the cost of clearance through a commercial firm may be significantly higher than the cost of a planned disposal collection. Arranging office clearance IT equipment removal before the lease exit is almost always cheaper than dealing with it through dilapidations. Decommissioning an office properly – accounting for every device before the keys are handed back – is both a compliance requirement and a commercial one.

How should you plan the IT side of a move or closure?

When should the IT audit and inventory take place?

Six to eight weeks before the move date is the target. That gives enough time to complete a full physical audit of every location where equipment is held, identify what moves with you, what gets redeployed internally, and what gets retired. It also gives enough time to book a collection, which for large volumes may need to be coordinated around the removal schedule. Our full list of what we collect covers the complete range of device types we handle, which can help scope the audit.

How do you decide what moves, what gets reused and what gets retired?

The decision framework is straightforward: anything moving to the new premises should be confirmed as fit for purpose and not carrying an outstanding data destruction obligation. Anything being redeployed to another user internally should go through the same process – a redeployment is not a disposal, but if the device holds data from a previous user it should be wiped before redeployment. Anything being retired should be segregated, logged against the asset register, and allocated to a certified disposal collection.

Devices that are borderline – in reasonable condition but not needed at the new site – should be assessed for refurbishment and reuse potential rather than automatically recycled. A device that can be refurbished and returned to use is a better environmental outcome than material recycling. How do you maintain chain of custody while equipment is in transit or storage?

Equipment being retired should not travel with the general move. It should be collected separately, by a certified provider with tracked vehicles, before or after the main removal date. Mixing retired IT with general move traffic creates accountability gaps: equipment can be misplaced, misidentified, or misdirected without anyone noticing until the audit trail is reconstructed.

If equipment needs to be temporarily stored before collection, it should be secured and logged. The chain of custody clock starts at the point the device is taken out of service, not at the point it is handed to the collector.

What do you do about devices held by home and hybrid workers?

A move or closure often surfaces a device accountability problem that has been quietly growing since hybrid working became the norm. Devices issued to remote employees who have since left, or devices that were taken home during the pandemic and never returned, are part of the estate for closing an office IT equipment project. They represent the same GDPR exposure as devices in the office, and closing a site without accounting for them leaves the exposure open.

The process for recovering these devices is the same as for any leaver offboarding: a tracked return, a data destruction certificate on processing, and a record against the asset register. Our guide to recovering laptops from remote and hybrid workers covers this process in detail, including how to handle non-returns and what to do with devices that have been sitting in storage. If return is not possible, remote wipe and documented evidence of the wipe is the minimum.

How do you handle printers, copiers and other kit with hidden storage?

Multifunction printers and copiers are consistently the most overlooked data-bearing devices in an office clearance. Most modern units have internal hard drives that store cached copies of documents scanned, printed, faxed, or copied through them. These drives are covered by the same data destruction obligations as laptop or server drives. Our printer recycling service covers secure processing of these devices, including data destruction on internal storage. Our monitor recycling service covers screens and display units that may have been overlooked in the clearance plan.

Other devices with non-obvious storage include photocopiers, EPOS systems, digital signage units, and some networking equipment. These should all be treated as data-bearing until confirmed otherwise.

What does a properly documented clearance look like?

Which certificates and waste documentation should you receive, and when?

For every collection, you should receive a Duty of Care Waste Transfer Note at the point of collection – signed by the driver and emailed to you the same day. A Certificate of Recycling for all items and a Certificate of Data Destruction for each data-bearing device follow usually within 72 hours of processing. For regulated sectors or where individual asset evidence is required, our enhanced service provides individual wiping reports at the asset level.

How does free collection work for a full office or site clearance?

Our free collection service applies for ten or more qualifying items – laptops, PCs, and servers for example in any combination. For a full office clearance involving multiple device types, including monitors, printers, servers, and networking equipment, the collection can cover the full estate in a single visit. Collections can be scheduled around the removal timeline, including outside standard working hours where access is restricted.

How do you close out the old building with evidence you can file?

The documentation package from a certified office move IT disposal collection gives you what you need to close out the building: a Waste Transfer Note confirming the collection took place and who carried it out, and a carbon offset certificate documenting the environmental impact of the collection. These documents should be filed against the move project record, not left in an inbox.

What should you keep for your asset register and audit trail?

Every device retired during the move should be reconciled against the asset register: the serial number, the disposal date, the collection reference, and the certificate number. This record is what closes the loop between the device that was issued, the device that was collected, and the evidence that it was destroyed. For organisations that need to demonstrate WEEE compliance or data protection compliance to an auditor, this reconciliation is the audit trail. Our guide to cataloguing and preparing IT assets before recycling covers how to structure that inventory before the collection takes place, which makes the post-move reconciliation considerably more straightforward.

Frequently asked questions

Can a collection be booked around our removal schedule?

Yes. We collect from UK mainland premises on a date that suits your timeline, including before, during, or after the main removal. For large clearances, we can discuss phased collections if the volume needs to be split across multiple visits.

What if some equipment has already been left behind at the old site?

We can still collect from a vacated premises provided access can be arranged. Contact us with the situation and we will confirm what is possible.

Do we need to do anything to devices before collection?

No. We carry out all data destruction as part of the service. Do not attempt to wipe devices before collection, as this may create an inconsistency in the audit trail.

We have a server room to decommission – can you handle that as part of the clearance?

Yes. Our server room decommissioning service covers full rack decommissioning, including servers, storage arrays, networking equipment, and cabling. We can schedule this separately from the general office clearance if access or timing requires it.

What about redundant IT equipment that has been in storage for months?

Stored equipment carries the same data risk as active equipment. If it has not been formally processed, it is still within scope for the clearance. Our redundant IT equipment collection service covers any age and condition of device.
